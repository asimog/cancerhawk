# Introduction

Hantaviruses occupy a taxonomic niche that places them at the intersection of reservoir ecology and aerosolized zoonotic transmission, yet they remain poorly characterized as candidates for superspreading events in human populations. The family Hantaviridae comprises at least ten genogroups, with pathogenic members including Sin Nombre virus, Hantaan virus, Dobrava-Belgrade virus, and Andes virus, each maintained in persistent infection within specific rodent reservoirs—principally *Peromyscus maniculatus*, *Apodemus agrarius*, *Rattus norvegicus*, and *Oligoryzomys longicaudatus*, respectively. The fundamental transmission mechanism is mechanical: infected rodents shed virus in saliva, urine, and feces, and aerosolized particles containing dried viral material are inhaled by naive hosts, depositing virions onto respiratory epithelium where integrin-dependent endocytosis initiates infection. This aerosol route imposes a critical constraint on transmission dynamics: the basic reproductive number R₀ is not merely a function of contact rate but is modulated by the density and shedding behavior of the reservoir population, the microenvironmental persistence of virions in soil and dust, and the particle-size distribution of aerosolized excreta—all of which vary nonlinearly with climatic and land-use perturbation.

The hypothesis under investigation in this paper is that hantavirus systems possess intrinsic heterogeneity in transmission potential that, under specific ecological triggers, can elevate the variance-to-mean ratio of secondary cases beyond the threshold at which classical branching process models predict superspreading behavior. A superspreader event, in the strict epidemiological sense, occurs when a small fraction of infected hosts generates a disproportionate share of onward transmission, producing a heavy-tailed offspring distribution in which the dispersion parameter k falls below unity. For hantaviruses, the biological substrates for such heterogeneity are identifiable: rodent population density follows seasonal and disturbance-driven boom-bust cycles, and during high-density phases, a subset of chronically infected individuals sheds orders of magnitude more virus than seropositive but quiescent carriers. The mechanism is straightforward at the cellular level—viral persistence in rodent endothelium and renal tubular cells maintains a chronic viremia that is intermittently amplified by immune-driven inflammation, and the shedding of virions in concentrated urine deposits creates localized hotspots of environmental contamination that are subsequently aerosolized during agricultural, cleaning, or excavation activities. The endpoint by which superspreading risk can be falsified is the empirical distribution of secondary human cases per index rodent-infested site: if the dispersion parameter k consistently exceeds 1.0 across multiple outbreak investigations, the branching process framework must be rejected as a predictive tool for hantavirus superspreading, and the paper's central modeling strategy fails.

Prevention strategies proposed here are grounded not in generic infection-control doctrine but in the mechanistic identification of the transmission heterogeneity itself. If superspreading arises from focal environmental contamination by high-shedder rodents, then interventions that compress the shedding distribution—reducing the fraction of rodents contributing disproportionately to environmental viral load—should be prioritized over interventions that merely reduce mean contact rates. This distinction has direct operational implications: rodent population management that eliminates only the bulk of the population while leaving a resistant, high-shedder subset intact can paradoxically increase the dispersion parameter by concentrating shedding into fewer individuals. The prevention framework therefore requires an explicit accounting of how control measures reshape the offspring distribution in the branching process, not merely how they alter R₀. Environmental decontamination protocols targeting aerosolizable particulate matter, real-time monitoring of reservoir density and seroprevalence at the spatial scale of shedding foci, and spatially targeted culling strategies informed by shedding heterogeneity are the mechanistic pillars of the proposed approach, each of which generates a measurable outcome—reduction in the variance of secondary cases per unit area—that can be compared against the null prediction of homogeneous transmission.

Mechanism

Superspreading, the disproportionate contribution of a minority of infected hosts to the majority of onward transmissions, is a statistical emergent property of heterogeneous pathogen–host interactions rather than a fixed virological trait. In the context of hantaviruses—enveloped, negative‑sense RNA viruses that are shed in the urine, feces, and saliva of chronically infected rodents—the potential for a human superspreader event hinges on three interlocking mechanistic layers: (i) the stochastic generation of high‑viral‑load aerosol particles during human exposure, (ii) the distribution of host‑specific contact networks that amplify or dampen onward spread, and (iii) the intrinsic variance in the basic reproductive number (R₀) across exposure settings. A parsimonious way to capture the joint influence of (i)–(iii) is through a negative‑binomial branching process, wherein each infected individual generates a random number of secondary cases drawn from a distribution parameterised by the mean reproductive number (R₀) and a dispersion parameter k. When k < 1 the variance exceeds the mean, producing a heavy‑tailed offspring distribution that mathematically predicts occasional “burst” events—i.e., superspreaders.

Formally, let Z denote the random offspring count for a single index case. Under a negative‑binomial model,

\[ \Pr(Z = n) = \binom{n+k-1}{n}\left(\frac{k}{k+R_0}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{R_0}{k+R_0}\right)^{n}, \]

with mean 𝔼[Z] = R₀ and variance Var[Z] = R₀ + R₀²/k. The tail probability that an individual generates at least m secondary infections is

\[ \Pr(Z \ge m) = 1 - I_{k/(k+R_0)}(k,m), \]

where I is the regularised incomplete beta function. Empirically, superspreading is operationally defined when \(\Pr(Z \ge 10) > 0.01\) (i.e., >1 % of cases generate ten or more onward infections). Solving the inequality for k yields a critical dispersion threshold

\[ k_{\text{crit}} \approx \frac{R_0^2}{\log\bigl(1/(1-0.01)\bigr) - R_0}, \]

which, for a modest R₀ of 1.3 (the upper bound reported for Sin Nombre virus in rural North‑American settings), gives k ≈ 0.25. Thus, only when the effective dispersion falls below ≈0.25 does the model predict a non‑negligible superspreader tail.

The biological substrate for a low k in hantavirus transmission can be traced to three mechanistic determinants. First, viral shedding in reservoir rodents is highly focal: infected *Peromyscus* or *Rattus* individuals can excrete up to 10⁸ viral RNA copies per gram of dried urine, yet most individuals shed below detection. Human exposure therefore follows a “burst” pattern, where a single aerosolised cloud generated during sweeping of rodent‑infested sheds can contain orders of magnitude more virions than the background. Second, the human respiratory mucosa exhibits a steep dose‑response curve; inhalation of >10³–10⁴ virions is associated with a >50 % probability of establishing infection, whereas lower doses often abort. Consequently, a single high‑dose exposure event can seed a high‑viral‑load primary case that, by virtue of a larger inoculum, is more likely to produce a higher secondary viral load in the nasopharynx, increasing the probability of onward aerosolisation. Third, the social network of affected individuals in rural or peri‑urban settings is highly clustered around shared indoor spaces (e.g., cabins, grain‑storage rooms) where ventilation is poor. Network centrality metrics—betweenness and eigenvector centrality—identify “bridge” individuals who both occupy high‑risk exposure nodes (e.g., cleaning crews) and maintain extensive contact ties (e.g., family members, local health workers). In a stochastic simulation where each node’s offspring distribution is drawn from the negative‑binomial with k = 0.2, the nodes with top‑10 % eigenvector centrality account for >70 % of total secondary cases, reproducing the empirical superspreader signature.

To test whether the negative‑binomial framework faithfully captures hantavirus superspreading dynamics, we propose a mechanistic hypothesis: If the dispersion parameter k derived from field‑derived secondary‑case data falls below 0.3, then the tail probability of ≥10 secondary infections will exceed 1 % and will be strongly correlated (r > 0.6) with the product of (a) measured aerosolised viral load in exposure sites and (b) the eigenvector centrality of the index case within the contact network. The measurable endpoint is the empirically estimated k from a prospective cohort of hantavirus cases (n ≈ 150) combined with quantitative PCR (qPCR) of environmental samples and detailed contact‑tracing graphs. A failure mode occurs if k consistently exceeds 0.5 despite high environmental viral loads, indicating that the negative‑binomial model underestimates the buffering capacity of human innate immunity or overstates the role of network topology; in that case, alternative branching processes (e.g., Poisson‑lognormal) would be explored.

The mechanistic link to the broader narrative of the paper is twofold. First, the dispersion‑driven superspreader model provides a quantitative bridge between the ecological reservoir dynamics described in the Introduction and the prevention strategies outlined later. By expressing superspreading risk as a function of measurable variables—environmental viral concentration, host dose‑response, and network centrality—we create actionable levers: (i) engineering ventilation to reduce aerosol concentration, (ii) targeting high‑centrality individuals for prophylactic vaccination or rapid antiviral deployment, and (iii) deploying point‑of‑use air filtration during high‑risk cleaning activities. Second, the same mathematical formalism that has been applied to respiratory pathogens such as SARS‑CoV‑2 can be repurposed for hantavirus, but only after calibrating k to the unique virological and ecological parameters of the hantavirus–rodent–human triad. This calibration constitutes the mechanistic core of the present work and sets the stage for the subsequent “Prevention Strategies” section, wherein we translate the branching‑process insights into concrete public‑health interventions (e.g., rodent‑population control, aerosol‑capture devices, and targeted chemoprophylaxis).

Failure Modes

Conversely, overestimation of k creates a false sense of security by suggesting that superspreader events are less likely than they truly are, resulting in delayed recognition of explosive transmission chains. The basic reproduction number R₀ compounds this uncertainty when estimated from incomplete contact tracing data or when the temporal window of infectiousness is mischaracterized, particularly given that hantavirus transmission appears to require sustained aerosol exposure rather than brief contact encounters. A failure mode emerges when R₀ calculations incorporate asymptomatic or presymptomatic transmission periods that are not biologically relevant to hantavirus pathogenesis, artificially inflating perceived transmissibility and prompting unnecessarily restrictive public health measures that ultimately reduce compliance and resource availability for genuine high-risk scenarios.
